# Module 4.2: Watermelon and Cantaloupe

## Two Melons, Two Worlds

*Watermelon and cantaloupe illustrate how structure shapes sensory experience.*

The watermelon (*Citrullus lanatus*) is a study in scale. A single fruit can weigh over 10 kilograms, yet its internal structure is remarkably consistent. The thick green rind, often striped or mottled, encloses a crisp flesh that ranges from deep pink to pale yellow depending on variety. What makes watermelon distinctive is its texture: the flesh is composed of large, thin-walled cells that rupture easily, releasing a flood of lightly sweet, refreshing juice. This cellular structure gives watermelon its characteristic crunch and explains why it feels so different from the dense, buttery flesh of other melons.@ramirez2021citrus

When you cut into a watermelon, notice how the seeds -- if present -- are arranged in a regular pattern following the fruit's internal architecture. Observe the gradient of colour from the white rind through the lighter flesh near the rind to the deepest colour at the centre. Taste the flesh from different parts of the fruit: the centre is typically sweetest, while the flesh near the rind is milder and slightly more vegetal.

Cantaloupe (*Cucumis melo var. cantalupensis*), also known as rockmelon, offers a completely different experience. The exterior is covered in a distinctive netted pattern -- a corky tissue that develops as the fruit matures. Inside, the orange flesh is dense, buttery, and intensely aromatic. The aroma of a ripe cantaloupe can fill a room, and this fragrance is a reliable indicator of ripeness and flavour quality.@harper2021sensory

The central cavity of a cantaloupe is filled with seeds and stringy fibres that must be scooped out before eating. This structural feature -- present in most melons but largely absent in modern seedless watermelons -- creates a natural division between the edible flesh and the inedible core. The contrast between the sweet, perfumed flesh and the bland, fibrous seed mass highlights how melons allocate resources between reproduction and palatability.

## Preservation Observation

Cut equal-sized cubes from a watermelon and a cantaloupe. Place half in the refrigerator and leave half at room temperature. Observe and photograph both sets after 2, 6, and 24 hours. Record:

- Changes in texture (firmness, juiciness)
- Changes in aroma intensity
- Any colour changes
- The development of off-flavours, if any

This exercise directly supports the preservation reflection component of Assessment 2.
