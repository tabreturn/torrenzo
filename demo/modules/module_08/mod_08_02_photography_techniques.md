# Module 8.2: Techniques for Documentation and Exhibition

## The Documentarian's Toolkit

*Good fruit photography is a balance of technical competence and observational intention.*

### Lighting

Lighting is the single most important factor in fruit photography. Poor lighting mutes colours, obscures texture, and creates distracting shadows. Good lighting reveals the subtle bloom on a blueberry, the glossy sheen of a cherry, the crystalline structure of a cut citrus segment.

For documentation purposes, diffuse natural light is ideal. Position your fruit near a large window but out of direct sunlight. A piece of white paper or foam board on the opposite side can bounce light back into the shadows, reducing contrast and revealing detail. For consistent results across multiple sessions, note the time of day and weather conditions -- or invest in a simple LED light panel that you can position identically each time.@harper2021sensory

For exhibition photography, you may want to be more creative with lighting. Side lighting emphasises texture and creates dramatic shadows. Backlighting makes translucent fruits (citrus segments, dragon fruit) glow. A dark background with a single light source can create a painterly, dramatic effect suitable for an exhibition.

### Composition

Documentation photographs should be straightforward and informative. The classic approach is a three-shot sequence: whole fruit, halved fruit, and a close-up of a distinctive feature (skin texture, seed arrangement, flesh colour). Use a plain background -- white, grey, or black -- to eliminate distractions. Include a scale reference: a ruler, a coin, or a familiar object that gives the viewer a sense of size.

Exhibition photographs can be more experimental. Consider:
- **Context**: Photograph the fruit in its environment -- on the tree, in a market, on a kitchen table.
- **Interaction**: Show hands peeling, cutting, or holding the fruit. Human presence adds scale and narrative.
- **Juxtaposition**: Place contrasting fruits together to highlight differences in colour, texture, or form.
- **Abstraction**: Extreme close-ups can transform familiar fruits into alien landscapes of texture and colour.

### Colour Accuracy

Colour is one of the most important sensory dimensions of fruit, and inaccurate colour undermines documentation. Digital cameras and phone cameras apply automatic white balance and colour processing that can shift hues in unpredictable ways.

For documentation, include a colour reference card or a known white object in at least one frame of each session. You can use this to correct colour in post-processing. For exhibition work, you may choose to emphasise or stylise colour, but understand that you are making an aesthetic choice, not a documentary one.

### Consistency Across a Collection

Your digital exhibition will include photographs of ten different fruits. Inconsistent photography -- different backgrounds, different lighting, different angles -- will make the exhibition feel disjointed. Consistent photography creates visual unity and signals professionalism.

Choose a consistent approach before you start shooting:
- Same background colour and texture for all fruits
- Same lighting setup for all fruits
- Same shooting angles (whole, cut, close-up) for all fruits
- Same post-processing (colour correction, cropping, sharpening) for all fruits

Document your choices so your exhibition partner can replicate them if you are dividing the photography work.

## Practice Shoot

Select three fruits from different families (e.g., citrus, berry, melon). Photograph each using two different approaches:

1. **Documentation style**: Plain background, diffuse lighting, three-shot sequence, colour reference, scale reference.
2. **Exhibition style**: Creative background, dramatic lighting, experimental composition, emotional or narrative intent.

Compare the results. Which approach serves each fruit better? When would you use each style?
