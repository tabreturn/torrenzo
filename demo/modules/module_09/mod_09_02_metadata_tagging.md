# Module 9.2: Metadata, Tagging, and Taxonomy

## The Architecture of a Collection

*Metadata is the invisible structure that makes a collection usable. Without it, a gallery is just a pile of pictures.*

### What is Metadata?

Metadata is "data about data" -- information that describes, explains, or locates a resource. In a fruit exhibition, metadata might include:

- **Descriptive metadata**: Fruit name, variety, photograph date, photographer
- **Structural metadata**: How entries relate to each other (e.g., "see also," "compare with," "part of the citrus sequence")
- **Administrative metadata**: File format, image dimensions, rights and permissions
- **Subject metadata**: Tags, keywords, categories that enable browsing and searching

Good metadata is consistent, comprehensive, and useful. It helps visitors find what they are looking for and discover connections they did not expect.@marshall2020catalogue

### Designing a Tagging System

A tagging system is a controlled vocabulary -- a set of terms you apply consistently across your collection. Useful tag categories might include fruit family, origin region, season, flavour profile, texture, colour, preservation suitability, and cultural significance.

The key to a good tagging system is consistency. Before you start tagging, agree with your partner on the exact terms you will use and what each term means. "Juicy" and "succulent" might seem interchangeable, but using both inconsistently will make your tags less useful for browsing.

### Taxonomy vs. Folksonomy

A taxonomy is a hierarchical classification system. In botany, fruits are classified by family, genus, and species. This is a taxonomy: a structured, authoritative system of categories and subcategories.

A folksonomy is a user-generated tagging system where anyone can apply any tag. Social media hashtags are a folksonomy: they emerge organically from community use rather than being imposed from above.

Your exhibition should primarily use a taxonomy -- a controlled, consistent set of tags that you define in advance. But you might also allow for a few curated folksonomic tags that capture subjective, experiential dimensions: "childhood memory," "surprisingly sweet," "worth the mess." These add personality and human connection to your metadata.

### Structuring Your Exhibition

Think of your ten fruits as a sequence, not just a list. How will visitors move through your exhibition? Common curatorial structures include:

- **Chronological**: From fruits you encountered first to those you encountered last
- **Thematic**: Grouping fruits by family, flavour, or cultural theme
- **Narrative**: A journey that builds from familiar to exotic, or from subtle to intense
- **Comparative**: Pairs of fruits presented side by side to highlight contrasts
- **Geographic**: A tour through the tropical regions of the world

Choose a structure that serves your story. A chronological sequence is easiest to execute but may not be the most engaging. A narrative or thematic structure rewards careful planning with a more compelling visitor experience.

### Navigation and Discovery

Beyond the basic sequence, how can visitors explore your exhibition? Consider:

- A **tag index** at the top of the page, allowing visitors to filter by family, region, or flavour profile
- **Cross-references** within entries: "If you enjoyed the mango, try the papaya..."
- A **visual overview** -- a grid of thumbnail images that lets visitors jump to any entry

These features make your exhibition interactive and reward exploration. They also demonstrate your curatorial skill: a well-structured collection is a pleasure to browse.

## Practice Activity

With your exhibition partner:

1. List the ten fruits you plan to include in your exhibition.
2. Design a tagging system with 6--8 categories and controlled vocabularies for each.
3. Tag your first three fruits using this system. Are the tags useful? Do they suggest interesting connections between fruits?
4. Decide on an exhibition structure (chronological, thematic, narrative, comparative, or geographic).
5. Arrange your ten fruits in your chosen sequence. Write a one-sentence rationale for the position of each fruit in the sequence.
6. Draft a 150-word curator's statement that introduces your exhibition and explains your curatorial approach.
