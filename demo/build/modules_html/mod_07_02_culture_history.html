Module 7.2: Symbolism, Trade, and Identity

Fruit as Cultural Object

The journey of tropical fruits from their places of origin to global supermarket shelves is a story of colonialism, trade, migration, and cultural exchange.

Symbolism and Mythology

Fruits appear in the mythology and religious traditions of nearly every culture. The mango is sacred in Hinduism and Buddhism, associated with love, fertility, and prosperity. Mango leaves are strung across doorways during festivals and weddings across India. The fruit appears in ancient Sanskrit texts and Mughal miniature paintings, where its golden flesh symbolises the sweetness of divine love.[1]

The coconut holds similar significance across the Pacific Islands, South Asia, and the Caribbean. In Hindu rituals, the coconut represents the human ego – hard on the outside, sweet within – and is broken as an offering to signify surrender. In Pacific Island cultures, the coconut palm is the "tree of life," providing food, drink, fibre, timber, and medicine. Every part of the tree has a use and a name.

The pomegranate, while not strictly tropical, illustrates how fruit symbolism travels across cultures. In Greek myth, Persephone's consumption of pomegranate seeds binds her to the underworld. In Jewish tradition, the pomegranate's 613 seeds (by some counts) correspond to the 613 mitzvot. In Chinese culture, the pomegranate symbolises fertility and abundance, its many seeds representing many offspring.[2]

Trade and Colonialism

The global distribution of tropical fruits is a direct legacy of colonial trade networks. The pineapple, native to South America, was carried to the Philippines by Spanish colonists in the 16th century, to Hawaii in the 18th century, and to Africa and Asia by Portuguese traders. By the 19th century, the pineapple had become a symbol of hospitality in Europe and North America – an exotic luxury that graced the tables of the wealthy.

The banana tells an even more dramatic story. Native to Southeast Asia, bananas were carried across the Indian Ocean by Austronesian traders, reached Africa by the first millennium CE, and crossed the Atlantic with the transatlantic slave trade. In the 20th century, the banana became the archetypal commodity crop, with vast plantations in Central America controlled by American corporations. The term "banana republic" – coined by O. Henry in 1904 – reflects the political instability created by this extractive industry.

The mango followed a similar path. Indian mango varieties were carried across the British Empire – to the Caribbean, East Africa, and the Pacific – by indentured labourers and colonial administrators. Today, mangoes are grown in over 100 countries, but the varieties found in Western supermarkets represent only a fraction of the thousands of cultivars grown in South Asia.

Cultural Identity

For diaspora communities, fruit can be a powerful connection to homeland and heritage. A Filipino migrant in London might seek out calamansi – a small citrus fruit essential to Filipino cuisine – as a taste of home. A Jamaican family in Toronto might grow ackee trees in their backyard, maintaining a connection to the national dish of Jamaica. These fruits carry memories, identities, and a sense of belonging.

Research Activity

Choose one tropical fruit with a rich cultural history (mango, coconut, banana, pineapple, durian, or pomegranate). Research its journey from its region of origin to its current global distribution. Consider:

  • Where was it first domesticated?
  • How did it travel? Who carried it, and why?
  • What symbolic meanings has it carried in different cultures?
  • How is it represented in art, literature, or popular media today?
  • For diaspora communities, what does this fruit signify?

Compile your findings into a 200-word cultural note suitable for inclusion in a digital exhibition entry.
