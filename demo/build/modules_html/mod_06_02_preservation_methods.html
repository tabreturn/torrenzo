Module 6.2: Drying, Freezing, and Sugar Preservation

Three Methods, Three Outcomes

Each preservation method transforms fruit through a different physical mechanism, producing distinctive sensory outcomes.

Drying (Dehydration)

Drying is the oldest method of fruit preservation and one of the simplest: remove water to inhibit microbial growth and enzymatic activity. But the sensory transformation is profound. As water evaporates, sugars concentrate, intensifying sweetness. The fruit shrinks and becomes chewy or leathery. Aromas change as volatile compounds escape or react with heat.[1]

Different fruits respond differently to drying. Mangoes dry beautifully, becoming intensely sweet and chewy, their flavour concentrated into something almost candy-like. Watermelon, with its 92% water content, shrinks to a fraction of its original volume and becomes crisp rather than chewy, its subtle flavour largely lost. Strawberries retain their colour well but lose much of their aroma.

Observation protocol for drying:

  1. Photograph and describe the fresh fruit.
  2. Slice uniformly (5mm thickness works well for most fruits).
  3. Dry at 60°C in an oven or food dehydrator, checking every 2 hours.
  4. Photograph and describe the dried fruit, noting changes in size, colour, texture, and flavour.

Freezing

Freezing preserves fruit by slowing enzymatic and microbial activity to near-zero. But it also causes physical damage: water inside cells expands as it crystallises, rupturing cell walls. When the fruit thaws, it often becomes soft, watery, and structurally compromised.[2]

Fruits with firm, dense flesh (mango, banana) survive freezing better than delicate fruits (berries, melon). The rate of freezing matters: rapid freezing produces smaller ice crystals and less cellular damage than slow freezing. This is why commercially frozen fruit often has better texture than fruit frozen in a home freezer.

Observation protocol for freezing:

  1. Photograph and describe the fresh fruit.
  2. Freeze on a tray (to prevent clumping) for 24 hours.
  3. Remove from freezer and photograph immediately.
  4. Allow to thaw completely at room temperature.
  5. Photograph and describe the thawed fruit, noting texture changes, water loss, and flavour changes.

Sugar Preservation

Sugar preserves fruit by osmosis: high sugar concentrations draw water out of microbial cells, inhibiting their growth. But sugar also transforms the fruit itself, drawing out its natural juices and replacing them with syrup. The result is often a softer, sweeter, more translucent fruit that takes on the character of the sugar solution.[1]

Sugar preservation includes jam-making, candying, and simple syrup maceration. Each produces different textures and flavour profiles, but all share the common principle of using sugar to alter water activity.

Observation protocol for sugar preservation:

  1. Photograph and describe the fresh fruit.
  2. Mix equal parts fruit and sugar by weight; let stand for 24 hours, stirring occasionally.
  3. Drain the fruit and reserve the syrup.
  4. Photograph and describe the preserved fruit and the syrup separately.
  5. Note changes in colour, translucency, texture, and flavour.

Comparative Exercise

Select one fruit and apply at least two preservation methods. Create a before-and-after comparison that documents changes across all sensory dimensions. This exercise directly prepares you for the preservation reflection in Assessment 2.
